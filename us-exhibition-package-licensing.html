Acquisition strategy / U.S. presentation

Start with the party that controls the finished exhibition.

For this shortlist, approach the producer or authorized U.S. distributor first. Your objective is to secure a repeatable exhibition package and permission to present it in the U.S. Ask the supplier to identify its authority, the scope of included rights and any approvals still required. A prior U.S. run demonstrates precedent; it does not establish current availability or authority for your proposed tour.

Your objectiveFirst contact and strategic reason

Publisher and local-agent rule: use them as the lead contact only when their mandate covers the relevant exhibition rights. Book, game, merchandise and live-experience rights may be controlled by different parties. A company’s title alone does not establish its authority.

Typical structure: IP rights holder → authorized producer → distributor or local presenter → venue and audience. Parties may combine roles or contract directly. Booking normally secures a defined presentation term; ownership of the underlying IP or permanent reuse rights should never be assumed.

Two workstreams / one investment decision

Qualify rights and feasibility together.

  1. Send a credible buyer brief.Provide company credentials, target cities, dates, venue parameters, budget range and intended operating model. A venue shortlist is enough for initial qualification.
  2. Establish the contracting route.Identify who supplies the package, who grants presentation rights, who receives payment and who obtains brand approvals. Request written evidence appropriate to that role.
  3. Test delivery in parallel.Review the technical rider, inventory, installation time, throughput, accessibility, freight and local fabrication requirements against candidate venues.
  4. Advance only when both workstreams clear.Resolve material rights and delivery gaps before major non-refundable venue, fabrication or marketing commitments.

Escalation rule: if the supplier cannot confirm its mandate, request an introduction to the rights holder or relevant agent. Avoid negotiating the same rights through competing channels until the authorized route is clear.

Rights scope / responsibility matrix

Ask for a written map of permissions.

For each use, record whether it is included, separately licensed, subject to approval or excluded; identify the responsible party and deadline.

  • Exact production, U.S. territory, cities, dates, operator and permitted venue types.
  • Public presentation, translations, adaptation, local fabrication, software and content playback.
  • Branding, promotional images, paid advertising, music and likenesses where applicable.
  • Sponsors, merchandise, visitor photography and recording.
  • Multi-city reuse, exclusivity, extensions, second copies and disposition of locally built assets.

Decision gate: resolve permissions that are essential to the business model before commitment. Merchandise or sponsorship income should enter the base case only when the relevant rights and economics are established.

Commercial discipline

Underwrite each city, then the tour.

Request comparable proposals covering fixed rental, revenue share, minimum guarantee plus royalty, or co-promotion where offered. Model the actual payment structure, including whether a guarantee is recoupable against royalties.

  1. Build the full local cost.Include venue, freight, installation, staffing, marketing, insurance, equipment, maintenance, contingencies and cash tied up in deposits.
  2. Stress-test attendance and timing.Use downside, base and upside scenarios. Assess net ticket yield, operating capacity, supplier deductions and opening delays.
  3. Evaluate the tour separately.Include inter-city transport, storage, downtime, refurbishment and any costs that cannot be reused. Multi-city revenue alone does not establish multi-city profitability.

Approval gate: proceed when the downside exposure, working-capital requirement and responsibilities fit your company’s limits.

Negotiation priorities

Buy operational certainty and useful flexibility.

  • Delivery: itemized inventory, acceptance criteria, support, training, maintenance and remedies for missing or late components.
  • Approvals: named decision makers, submission requirements and agreed review timelines.
  • Economics: defined royalty base, deductions, settlement timing, reporting and audit provisions.
  • Tour options: negotiate city additions, extensions or future dates with clear conditions rather than paying upfront for untested expansion.
  • Risk allocation: document cancellation, postponement, damage, insurance and rights-related responsibilities.

Recommended sequence: shortlist → qualify authority and availability → assess technical and financial fit → agree scope and terms → contract → approvals and installation → opening and reporting.

The first call

Confirm authority before major commitments.

  1. Confirm the U.S. mandate.Ask who can grant this exact exhibition for your cities, dates and operator. Request the authority chain and any required rights-holder approvals.
  2. Define what “package” includes.List content masters, design drawings, scenery, objects, AV, software, marketing assets, manuals, training and maintenance.
  3. Separate supply from local work.Which components ship? Which can be fabricated locally? Who installs, approves and operates the show? Can one package be reused in several cities?
  4. Set the territory and term.Confirm multi-city scope, any city radius restrictions, minimum runs, exclusivity, marketing approvals and extension options.
  5. Price the complete engagement.Ask for fixed fees, guarantees, royalties, revenue share and all additional operating obligations in one comparable proposal.
